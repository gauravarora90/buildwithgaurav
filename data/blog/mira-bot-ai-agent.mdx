I have a confession: I was spending at least 90 minutes a day just _checking things_.

GitHub PRs waiting for review. CleverTap funnels to eyeball. DigitalOcean cron jobs that may or may not have run. Slack threads asking for status updates I had to manually go compile. Every tool had its own dashboard, its own login, its own mental context I had to load and unload all day long.

I'm a Principal Product Engineer at a logistics tech startup. We run 50+ microservices. There is no shortage of things to monitor. And for a while, I just accepted that context-switching was the tax I paid for working across a complex stack.

Then I got tired of paying it.

## The Problem Was Never the Tools

Here's what I realized: none of the individual tools were bad. GitHub is great. CleverTap is powerful. DigitalOcean works. The problem was _me_ — a human being — acting as the integration layer between all of them. I was the glue. And glue doesn't scale.

What I actually wanted was simple: tell me what I need to know, when I need to know it, in one place. Don't make me go looking.

So I built Mira Bot.

## What Mira Bot Actually Does

Mira is an AI-powered Telegram agent. She lives in a Telegram chat — accessible from my phone, my laptop, anywhere — and she talks to the tools so I don't have to.

Here's what a typical morning looks like now:

I open Telegram. I type `/prs` and Mira responds with a clean summary of open pull requests across our repos — who's waiting on review, what's been idle for more than 24 hours, which ones have merge conflicts. Not a dump of raw JSON. An actual readable summary, generated by OpenAI, that I can act on in 30 seconds.

I type `/analytics` and she pulls the latest CleverTap funnel reports — user activation rates, drop-off points, feature engagement — and surfaces the one or two numbers that moved meaningfully since yesterday. No logging into CleverTap. No navigating dashboards. Just signal.

I type `/crons` and she checks the DigitalOcean cron job logs and tells me what ran, what failed, and what hasn't been heard from in a suspicious amount of time.

That's it. Three commands. The 90 minutes became 5.

## The "Aha Moment"

The first time it really worked — I mean _really_ worked — I was on a train, no laptop, getting pinged by a teammate asking about a stalled PR. I pulled out my phone, typed `/prs`, and read back a GPT-generated paragraph that named the exact PR, explained why it was blocked, and suggested who should look at it.

I forwarded Mira's response to my teammate. Problem solved. On a train. In 45 seconds.

That was the moment I stopped thinking of Mira as a side project and started thinking of her as infrastructure.

## The Architecture (Kept Real)

Mira is built on a fairly straightforward stack, but the design decisions matter.

**The interface is Telegram.** I chose Telegram over Slack or a web dashboard for one reason: it's always open. I'm not going to build a new internal tool that people have to remember to visit. Telegram is already where async communication happens. Meet people where they are.

**The brain is OpenAI.** Raw API data from GitHub, CleverTap, and DigitalOcean is fed into GPT as context, with a system prompt that tells it to behave like a senior engineer summarizing information for a peer. The quality of the output is almost entirely determined by the quality of that prompt. I spent more time on prompt engineering than on any other part of the system.

**The integrations are thin wrappers.** GitHub has a solid REST API. CleverTap has webhooks and a reporting API. DigitalOcean's API exposes cron run history. I wrote small, focused Python functions for each — not frameworks, not abstractions, just functions that fetch data and return structured dicts.

**The command routing is dead simple.** A Telegram bot listens for messages. If the message starts with `/`, it routes to the appropriate handler. The handler fetches data, formats it as a prompt, calls OpenAI, and sends the response back. The whole core loop is maybe 200 lines of code.

The thing that surprised me: the hard part wasn't any of this. The hard part was _deciding what the agent should and shouldn't do_.

## What Building AI Agents Actually Teaches You

Everyone is building AI agents right now. Most of them are impressive demos that don't survive contact with real workflows. Here's what I learned from building one that actually stuck:

**Scope is everything.** Mira does three things. Extremely well. I deliberately never added a fourth thing that would bloat her into something I had to maintain instead of trust.

**The AI is a formatter, not a decision-maker.** Mira summarizes and surfaces. She doesn't act. She doesn't merge PRs, she doesn't trigger deployments, she doesn't send emails. The moment you give an agent write access, you've introduced a failure mode that can cost you at 2am. I made that decision deliberately and I'd make it again.

**Latency kills adoption.** If the bot takes 12 seconds to respond, you stop using it. I optimized aggressively — caching API responses, batching requests, streaming where possible. Sub-3-second responses, most of the time.

**Reliability over features.** Every time Mira gave me a wrong answer — a stale cache, a prompt that misread the data — trust eroded a little. I learned to treat accuracy as the top-priority feature. A bot that's right 70% of the time is worse than no bot at all.

## Where Mira Is Going

I'm exploring a few things: proactive alerts (Mira pings _me_ when something anomalous happens, instead of waiting to be asked), integration with our deployment pipeline so she can report on release health, and eventually some lightweight write operations — like leaving a comment on a PR when it's been idle for 48 hours.

But I'm going slowly. Because Mira works right now. And "works right now" is a thing worth protecting.
